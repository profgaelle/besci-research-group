sections:
  - id: why
    nav: Why it matters
    title: Employers increasingly look for resilience in the face of uncertainty
    text: |
      Work changes quickly, and outcomes are often unclear. The World Economic Forum’s [*Future of Jobs Report 2025*](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf) places resilience, flexibility and agility among the fastest-growing skills employers seek.

      Kingston University’s [*Future Skills 2025*](https://assets.kingston.ac.uk/m/32c8151479564c48/original/20250617_FutureSkills2025_report-1.pdf) report, based on a YouGov survey of more than 2,000 UK business leaders, finds that resilience, creativity and adaptability have all grown in importance to employers since 2023. Resilience is also one of the graduate attributes Kingston now builds into every undergraduate degree.

  - id: question
    nav: The question
    title: We know little about how this capacity develops
    text: |
      Most research studies resilience in organisations and systems. In individuals, it is usually measured by questionnaire, as a stable trait. So we know little about how a person becomes more resilient, or what that change looks like in behaviour.

      GRACE asks whether creative practice offers conditions for that development, and whether the change can be observed in how people act and move.

  - id: approach
    nav: Our approach
    title: Three fields, one question
    text: GRACE brings together three fields not previously combined in this way, each led by one of the project’s co-leads.
    strands:
      - title: Behavioural science
        who: Professor Gaëlle Vallée-Tourangeau, Kingston Business School
        text: What changes as people learn to work with uncertainty, and what counts as evidence of that change?
        colour: blue
      - title: Creative practice
        who: Professor Marcus O’Dair, Director of the Design, Arts and Creative Practice Knowledge Exchange and Research Institute
        text: Can creative practices improve people’s resilience in the face of uncertainty?
        colour: yellow
      - title: Computer vision
        who: Professor Dimitrios Makris, School of Computer Science and Mathematics
        text: Can cameras capture the movements and gestures that might show how people engage with uncertainty?
        colour: turquoise
    text_after: |
      Earlier research suggests that people differ in how they engage with physical materials when a problem is uncertain, and that more active engagement goes with more successful problem-solving. Movement and gesture may therefore carry cues about how people engage with uncertainty. Our pilot treats this as an open question to test.

      Over six months, we run three workshops, a small studio pilot and a synthesis event. Together they will set out a research agenda for a larger programme.

      The project team also includes Christine Mulrooney, Project Coordinator, Kingston University, and Olly Hixon-Fisher, Research and Innovation Associate, School of Computer Science and Mathematics.
